How to test your water before you buy anything

By the Test First Water team · Updated Nov 19, 2026 · 7 min read · No hands-on testing claimed: we compare published specs, policies, and certifications.

Short answer: On city water, start with your utility's free annual Consumer Confidence Report and ask the utility for your hardness number. On a private well, the CDC says to test at least once a year for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH, using a state-certified lab.

Every good water decision starts with a number. Without one, you're buying based on a sales pitch or a scary demo. Here's how to get real numbers for little or no money.

If you're on city water: read your Consumer Confidence Report

The EPA requires community water systems to send customers a Consumer Confidence Report, an annual drinking water quality report, by July 1 each year (EPA). If you can't find yours, the EPA has an online search tool on that page, or you can call your water supplier.

What to look for:

  • Disinfectant type. Chlorine or chloramine? It matters when you pick a carbon filter, because some filters are sold in separate chloramine versions.
  • Hardness. Often listed in mg/L or grains per gallon (gpg). If it isn't in the report, call the utility and ask. Divide mg/L by 17.1 to get gpg.
  • Any violations and the contaminants detected, compared with the legal limit.
Lead is the big exception. The EPA says the most common sources of lead in drinking water are lead pipes, faucets, and fixtures, and that lead service lines are typically the most significant source in homes that have them (EPA). Your utility's report can't tell you what your own pipes add. If your home is older, test water from your own tap through a certified lab.

If you're on a private well: test every year

EPA rules for public water systems don't apply to private wells, so the owner is responsible for testing. The CDC and EPA both say to test at least once a year for:

  • Total coliform bacteria
  • Nitrates
  • Total dissolved solids
  • pH

The CDC also says to ask your local health department what else is common in your area. That could be arsenic, lead, radium, VOCs, or pesticides. Test again after flooding or well repairs, if the taste, color, or smell changes, or if someone in the home becomes pregnant or a child moves in.

For equipment sizing, add these to the lab order: hardness, iron, manganese, and hydrogen sulfide (rotten-egg smell). Those decide which filter or softener you need. They're also the conditions some makers exclude from warranty coverage, as you'll see in our SpringWell review.

Where to get a real test

  1. Your local or state health department. Many test well water for bacteria and nitrate, or keep a list of certified labs.
  2. A state-certified drinking water lab. The EPA keeps a page linking every state's certification program and certified lab list (EPA).
  3. Mail-in kits sold by water companies. These are convenient, but you're asking a seller to diagnose a problem it sells the fix for. For example, SpringWell lists a well water test (Level 1 + Bacteria) at $169 (price checked Nov 19, 2026 on the maker's site; prices change often). Compare it with a certified lab before you buy.
  4. Hardness test strips. Fine for a ballpark hardness number, but not for health contaminants.

What a "free in-home water test" really is

Florida's Attorney General warns that sellers offering "free home water testing" may add tablets or drops to your water. When the water changes color, they say it's polluted. The AG says that in almost all of these cases, any water, even spring water, would "fail" the test (Florida AG). Minnesota's Department of Health describes the same tactic. The "tests" may only show normal minerals (MDH). More in our sales red flags guide.

Quick questions

How often should I test well water?

The CDC and EPA say at least once a year for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH, and again after flooding, repairs, or any change in taste, color, or smell.

Does my city water report show lead from my pipes?

No. The EPA says lead usually comes from lead service lines, faucets, and fixtures, so test water from your own tap if your home might have them.

How do I convert mg/L hardness to grains per gallon?

Divide mg/L (as calcium carbonate) by 17.1. For example, 171 mg/L is 10 gpg.
