Door-to-door water sales: red flags and how to back out

By the Test First Water team · Updated Nov 19, 2026 · 7 min read · No hands-on testing claimed: we compare published specs, policies, and certifications.

Short answer: You can say no, and you can usually undo a yes. Under the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule, a covered sale made at your home for $25 or more can be canceled until midnight of the third business day after the sale, and the seller has to give you the cancellation form. Get the pitch in writing, get your own test, and get two other quotes.

The pitch, and what state officials say about it

Minnesota's Department of Health lists the common moves (MDH):

  • Implying they work with your city or the health department. MDH says to ask for a named contact at that agency and call them yourself.
  • Reciting contamination stories from elsewhere, whether or not they affect your water.
  • In-home "tests" that may only show naturally occurring minerals, plus claims that water should contain "zero" of anything.
  • Misrepresenting drinking water standards to imply your water is unsafe.
  • A "one-time" discount on a unit that's actually sold at a heavily inflated price.
  • "Maintenance-free" claims. MDH says all treatment units need maintenance.

Florida's Attorney General adds the color-change "free test" (almost any water fails it), prize promotions that require buying a unit, and false claims of government approval. An EPA registration number only means the product was registered, not that the EPA tested or approved it (Florida AG).

Red flags checklist

  • They won't leave a written price, model number, and contract to review.
  • The price drops a lot when you hesitate. A Reddit user in the Orlando area described a pitch that fell from about $11,000 to $3,500 from a company with no website, phone, or card. That's one account, but it's the classic pattern.
  • They push financing before you've seen the total price.
  • They can't name a certification (NSF/ANSI 42, 53, 44, 55, or 58) for the exact model, or the certification isn't on NSF's or WQA's listing.
  • They claim the government requires the system.
  • "Today only."

Your three-day right to cancel

The FTC's Cooling-Off Rule covers door-to-door sales of $25 or more at your home, or $130 or more at temporary locations like hotel rooms or fairgrounds (16 CFR 429.0). The seller must (16 CFR 429.1):

  • give you a dated receipt or contract telling you that you may cancel before midnight of the third business day after the sale;
  • give you two copies of a "Notice of Cancellation" form, and tell you about the right out loud;
  • refund your payments within 10 business days after getting your cancellation.

To cancel, sign and date the notice (or write your own letter) and mail or deliver it before the deadline. Keep a copy and proof of mailing. Some states add more protection, so check with your state attorney general.

What to do instead

  1. Thank them and take a brochure. Don't let anyone "test" your water in your kitchen.
  2. Get real numbers: your city's report or a certified lab (how).
  3. Decide what problem you're solving: hardness, taste, or well contaminants.
  4. Get at least two written quotes. MDH suggests calling other water treatment companies for estimates.
  5. Already signed? Use the three-day cancel. Report deceptive sales to your state attorney general.

Quick questions

Can I cancel a water softener I bought from a door-to-door salesman?

Usually yes. The FTC Cooling-Off Rule lets you cancel a covered home sale of $25 or more until midnight of the third business day, and the seller must give you a cancellation form and refund within 10 business days.

Are free in-home water tests legit?

Treat them as a sales tool. Florida's Attorney General says color-change tests used by some sellers would make almost any water, even spring water, look contaminated. Use your utility report or a state-certified lab.

Does the government require home water treatment?

No. Florida's Attorney General calls claims that agencies require or recommend household purifiers false, and an EPA registration number doesn't mean EPA approval.
