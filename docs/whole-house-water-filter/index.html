Whole-house water filters for city water

Short answer: On city water that already meets EPA limits, a whole-house filter is mostly a comfort upgrade: less chlorine taste and smell at every tap. Check your utility's report first. If it uses chloramine, buy a filter sold for chloramine. If lead is your worry, a whole-house carbon tank isn't the answer. Test your tap and look at a point-of-use filter certified for lead.

Do you need one?

Minnesota's health department says most residents on public water don't need home treatment to protect their health, and treatment should be bought only to fix a specific water issue (MDH). Good reasons to buy a whole-house filter: you dislike the chlorine taste or smell, or you want sediment out before it reaches appliances. Bad reason: a salesperson's color-change demo.

Certifications, decoded

From NSF:

  • NSF/ANSI 42: aesthetic effects, like chlorine, taste, and odor. Covers whole-house (point-of-entry) systems too.
  • NSF/ANSI 53: a contaminant with a health effect, like lead.
  • NSF/ANSI 58: reverse osmosis, usually under-sink.
  • NSF/ANSI 401: up to 15 "emerging" contaminants.

Watch the wording. "Tested to" a standard is not the same as "certified to" it, and a certified tank or housing material (NSF/ANSI 61) doesn't mean the filter's performance is certified. Look up the exact model on NSF's listings or WQA's Gold Seal directory.

Before you click buy

  • Match flow to your home. Bigger homes need higher flow ratings, or you'll feel the pressure drop.
  • Budget for install. The Reddit buyer in our SpringWell review paid about $800 for a plumber on top of the equipment. Ask for a written quote.
  • Read the return policy. Many "money-back" promises exclude installed systems.
  • Price the replacement filters, not just the system.

Quick questions

Is a whole house water filter worth it on city water?

Usually only for taste, odor, or sediment. If your utility meets EPA limits, a whole-house filter is a comfort upgrade, not a health requirement. Check your Consumer Confidence Report first.

What does NSF/ANSI 42 mean?

It certifies reduction of aesthetic contaminants such as chlorine, taste, and odor. NSF/ANSI 53 covers contaminants with health effects, like lead.

Will a whole-house carbon filter remove chloramine?

Not all are sold for it. If your utility uses chloramine, choose a model the maker sells specifically for chloramine and check its certification.
